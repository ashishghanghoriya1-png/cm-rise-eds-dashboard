Executive Summary
This report is the full analytical record of the EDS diagnostic. It documents what sixty government middle school teachers reported about four CM RISE Teacher Professional Development (TPD) interventions, how those accounts were analysed, what alternative explanations were tested, and what the findings imply for the next planning cycle.
The study examined four TPD interventions: Subject-Specific In-Person Training (IPT), Digital Courses (DIKSHA and iGOT Karmayogi), Peer Learning Communities (Shaikshik Samvaad, or CLSS), and Classroom Observation and Mentoring (CRO). It was conducted through sixty in-depth interviews with government middle school teachers with record of participation in combination of TPD interventions, each accompanied by independent note-taker observation. The central question that the exercise attempted to unpack are the teachers’ experience, perception, expectations and concerns in their professional development ecosystem/ programme.
Summary of findings
In-Person Training (IPT)
IPT was examined are four specific themes: teachers' general attitude toward and awareness of subject in-person training that happened in year 2025, including what worked and what needs improvement; what content get retained with teacher and they report to be applying in classrooms and finally, a deliberate deep-dive into follow-up activities i.e., YTL, Webex, and SKG/Y, specifically because participation in these has been low, to understand how teachers view them and where the barrier to participation actually sits; and feedback on training resources, both whether they reach teachers at all and what teachers say once they do.
In results, IPT is valued by most teachers who attend it (56 of 60) and 52 of those find it useful. However, the link to classroom practice is inconsistent, and further, tiered classroom learning transfer analysis confirms classroom application in only 24 of 56 cases (43%).
Follow-up activity is where the participation concerns this study set out to investigate is most clearly confirmed: reach is low across all three channels (25 of 60 attend any of them at all), and where teachers do attend, engagement is often passive. Materials are valued when received, but 19 of 60 teachers report never receiving them at all- a distribution gap this study can describe but not fully explain from teacher-side evidence alone and may further need conversation with administrative officials.
Digital Courses (DIKSHA)
DIKSHA Digital courses were examined across six themes: general attitude toward their usefulness for professional development; the engagement pattern-  when teachers do courses, for how long, and whether the motivation is self-driven or externally imposed; how much course content teachers actually retain, as a proxy for depth of engagement; feedback on the platform and content, essentially a user survey; feedback on the communication channel through which teachers learn a course exists; and general suggestions for improvement.
Teachers’ awareness and participation have been fairly attained as 50 of 60 teachers have done some digital courses predominately in DIKSHA (40) and Igot (30). Teachers hold a broadly positive view of the online format- 38 of 60 rate it effective. Engagement is concentrated outside school hours within a narrow weekly time budget (65% report two hours or less), and awareness travels almost entirely through administrative WhatsApp channels.
Interactive mid-course quizzes followed by animated videos and assesments are well appreciated features. However, on the content recall front 23 of 50 teachers were able to recall course name or topic but hardly any internal content matter.
Shaikshik Samvaad (CLSS)
CLSS was examined for six themes: teacher perception of the peer learning format and reasons; the participation vs engagement pattern- what teachers remember attending, and which topics and themes stayed with them; classroom learning transfer; pattern and format of CLSS session learning cascade to non-participating teachers; reasons for low attendance recording complaince and general feedback for improvement.
In findings, CLSS is the strongest-performing intervention in the study by it’s participating teachers report on applying or transfering learnings to the classrooms: 34 of 53 attendees (64%) show confirmed classroom transfer. Opportunity to interact and learn through peers is the most popular reason of acceptance and enthusiasm for teachers.
Prominence of informal channel or rather lack of any formal channel or format to cascade CLSS monthly learnings to non-participating teachers in school emerged an area that need more work. Alongwith, issue of non-seriousness regarding attendance recording compliance rather than awareness will further need to be addressed.
Classroom Observation and Mentoring (CRO)
CRO or teacher mentoring has not yet been formally rolled out, so this study's role was a general exploration rather than a hypothesis-driven evaluation: understanding what teachers expect from classroom observation, what the design needs to be mindful of, and where their concerns concentrate.
Teachers currently associate classroom observation with inspection rather than support- 12 of 60 do not see it as professional development at all and converge on a small number of specific, actionable preferences for what a supportive model should look like: private and dignified feedback, solution-focused rather than evaluative guidance, an observer credible in the teacher's own subject, and, for a meaningful minority, co-teaching as a genuinely different model worth its own design decision.
1 · Background
CM RISE TPD programme was launched on 1 May 2020 as a continuity-of-training response during COVID-19 school closures, delivered by the Rajya Shiksha Kendra, Government of Madhya Pradesh in partnership with Peepul. It began as digital training on the national DIKSHA platform has since developed into a blended model that combines in-person subject training, self-paced digital courses and cluster-level peer learning communities (Shaikshik Samwaad). The programe is delivered to teachers across MP in the cascaded model with the support of district, block and cluster administrative and academic support.
While this model enables scale, it limits direct engagement with teachers and reduces visibility into how interventions are experienced at the field implementation and then at the classroom level. Current monitoring and feedback loops provide valuable implementation data. However, to design more responsive and contextually relevant interventions, there is a need for deeper understanding of teacher experiences, motivations, constraints, and expectations along with perspectives from academic officials across levels of the system. EDS exercise is therefore conceived as a structured diagnostic to strengthen the design and delivery of TPD interventions for the upcoming planning cycle.
The purpose of this exercise is to systematically examine the MP CPD ecosystem to:
Validate key assumptions underlying each intervention
Understand teacher’s experiences, expectation and perception with respect to TPD engagement.
Identify implementation gaps within the cascaded structure
Generate actionable insights for intervention redesign and planning
Scope of this report
Methodology- process to theme and framework identification, tool development and analysis framework including the teacher’s learning transfer to classroom coding framework.
Each finding with its supporting evidence, including the underlying mechanism, the distribution of evidence quality, and the alternative explanations considered before a finding was accepted.
Negative case analysis, documenting the teachers and evidence that do not fit each main claim, with a stated confidence level for each finding.
Teacher testimony, presented at length and in teachers' own words where it clarifies a finding.
A complete set of intervention wise recommendations.
Next proposed steps for the study.
2 · Methodology
2.1  Sample and its logic
The sampling design was developed internally by Peepul and agreed with RSK. It is a stratified random sample built on the one teacher attribute that administrative records capture reliably: participation/attendance. As subject training in 2025-26 year was one compulsory intervention with maximum attendance record available to RSK, it got considered to be the population for the sampling exercise.Teachers were categorised into three engagement/participation groups based on their participation in peer learning (aka CLSS) sessions, completion of digital courses, and attendance in in-person subject training:
The sampling frame was defined as teachers who had participated in at least one activity of each of three types in 2025-26: at least one of five CLSS sessions, at least one of two digital courses (PLC and PSP4), and subject training. This population totals 28,193 teachers, distributed across four participation categories:
Sixty interviews were drawn, with a target of twenty per participation category, selected at random within each category from the eligible list. Backup teachers were pre-selected to cover non-response. Following the initial draw, the distribution was reviewed for gender and district balance, and a small number of alternates were substituted from under-represented groups.
Why the sample size of 60: According to the study, the Law of Data Saturation (by SAGE Field Methods), after conducting 12 interviews within a specific group, the discovery of new themes drops to near zero. Hence, conducting 20 interviews under a specific engagement category, we exceed the scientific requirement for data saturation by 66%.

2.2  Interview method and the observation rule
Each interview was conducted by a trained data collector accompanied by a note-taker. The note-taker recorded a parallel observational account covering the teacher's manner, apparent recall, and consistency, and noted any divergence between what was claimed and what appeared credible.
Each interview ideally timed for 1-1.30 hrs and upon respondents’ consent audio recorded for the data entry purpose. Interviewer and note-takers would tally their notes and observation post interview and before recording the data in Kobo tool to ensure quality and reliable data.
2.3  Development of the EDS Interview Tool
The interview tool for the Ecosystem Diagnostic Study was developed through an iterative, internally-grounded process, designed to ensure that the questions asked were both analytically meaningful and practically answerable by government middle school teachers in the programme and intervention’s context.
The process began with focused internal team discussions aimed at mapping the areas of genuine uncertainty within the programme- specifically, aspects of teacher experience, engagement, and classroom transfer that existing administrative data and monitoring mechanisms do not capture. Rather than starting from a generic research framework, the team asked a prior question: what do we not yet know about how our interventions are working on the ground, and what would it be useful to know in order to improve programme design, implementation, and classroom reach? This exercise surfaced the thematic areas that became the backbone of the tool.
These themes were then developed into draft questions informed by a secondary review of relevant literature on teacher professional development, peer learning communities, and classroom transfer, drawing on established frameworks while keeping the language and focus grounded in the specific interventions under study: In-Person Training, DIKSHA digital courses, Shaikshik Samwaad, and Classroom Observation and Mentoring.
The draft tool was shared with RSK and SCERT stakeholders for review and input, ensuring alignment with state programme priorities and that the areas of inquiry were relevant to planning decisions at the system level.
Prior to full deployment, the tool was piloted with a small group of teachers to test clarity, flow, and the appropriateness of question framing for the target respondent group. Feedback from this pre-testing phase informed revisions to question wording, sequencing, and the design of probes particularly for sections where teacher recall or comfort with the subject required more careful scaffolding.
The final tool reflects these successive iterations: grounded in programme reality, validated with state stakeholders, and refined through field feedback before the full interview cycle began.

2.5  The learning transfer coding framework
Attendance and completion records indicate whether a teacher was present or finished a course; they do not indicate whether anything from that engagement changed classroom practice. This framework was developed to close that gap, establishing a consistent evidence standard for confirming genuine classroom transfer, tiered according to the strength of the available evidence. Applying this standard uniformly across interventions allows the study to report, with a defensible degree of confidence that alongwith participation, classroom practice was also demonstrably affected. In this way, the framework moves the analysis from a description of participation to an evidenced account of impact.
2.5.1 Two governing rules
The first is a participation gate. A teacher who did not attend an intervention cannot have transfer coded for that intervention. For e.g., the 66 percent (means how many out of 53) figure of classroom transfer is calculated over 53 attendees, not the full sample of 60.
The second is a three-tier evidence scale, applied once a teacher has passed the participation gate.
The requirement for independent confirmation at Tier 1 addresses self-report bias directly. The study's triangulation shows that teachers both overstate and understate their own technique use relative to independent observation, so the strongest transfer claims are constructed to not depend on teacher testimony alone. For this reason the report generally cites three figures rather than one: the Tier 1 rate (strong evidence), the combined Tier 1 and 2 rate (moderate evidence or better), and the all-tier rate (any evidence).
2.5.2 Three cross-cutting specificity codes
Three additional codes apply across the tiers: subject_specific_transfer, marking a technique linked to a named curriculum topic; transfer_habit_formation, marking application described as daily or systematic; and peer_as_transfer_source, marking a technique learned from a peer at a session rather than from the facilitator. The last of these is central to the CLSS mechanism finding developed in Section 4.3.
2.5.3 Adaptation to other interventions
This same framework was also applied to IPT, with one change: instead of asking whether a technique came from a peer, we correlated whether it depended on receiving printed training materials or happened without them.
The same framework was not applied to Digital Courses for two reasons. First, no question in the interview ever asked how a teacher used digital course content in their own classroom. Second, and more importantly, most of what was on offer this year wasn't really the kind of content that question could be asked about: three courses were early-childhood (ECE) courses, which don't apply to this study's middle-grade teachers; PLC, the most-enrolled course, was an orientation to the Shaikshik Samvaad platform, not exactly classroom teaching content; and the two targeted-instruction courses launched in the last month of the year, too late for classroom use to be realistic. So instead of transfer, what we measured for digital courses is recall:- can a teacher name or describe a course topic after finishing it.
2.6  Triangulation architecture
Interview findings were checked against three interventions’ secondary sources. These sources covers teachers more than EDS sampled sixty teachers but like EDS sample, represents 6-8 grades school teachers. Hence, emerged patterns and correlation can easily be generalized across population.
On the reliability caveat for the classroom observation exercise: Area 1 figures (teaching process) are used with confidence given their strong reliability score. Area 2 figures (classroom culture, norms) are weaker on this measure and are reported as indicative rather than precise. Stating this distinction is intended to make clear which observed figures carry more analytical weight, not to cast doubt on the exercise as a whole.
2.6  Limits
Descriptive, not causal. The study documents association rather than causation. Teachers who attend more CLSS sessions may already be more motivated; the transfer rate may partly reflect who attends rather than the effect of attending.
Recall window. Interviews took place five to eleven months after the training events under discussion. This affects IPT, a single distant event, more than CLSS, which is monthly and more recent.
Dependence on self-report below Tier 1. Tiers 2 and 3 rest on teacher testimony. The tier system controls for this only at Tier 1.
Single state, single programme. The findings are specific to the CM RISE context and should be treated as hypotheses for testing elsewhere, not as established conclusions.

3.1 · Caveats on this comparison.
Caveats considered while analysing this section are as below-
First, course and session participation as reported by teachers is self-reported, and depends on the individual teacher's ability to recall an event that may have occurred several months before the interview.
Second, the sample itself was drawn from legitimate backend records- attendance record and course-completion logs not from self-report. Where a teacher's interview shows less participation than their backend category implies, this is treated as a probable recall gap rather than evidence of non-participation, since the record that placed the teacher in that category is independent of what they later recalled.
Third, the reverse asymmetry does not hold to the same degree. Where a teacher reports more participation than their category implies, this may reflect a genuine gap in the backend record since backend records are themselves incomplete for some activities such as IPT and CLSS doesn’t have 100% success rate in fetching teachers attendance record.
. Fourth, the digital criterion specifically required a defined methodology, set out below, because of a gap in what the original interviews captured.
The sampling criteria define the digital component by two named courses, PLC and PSP4, not a general count of digital activity: High requires both, Medium and Low-2 require at least one, Low-1 requires neither.
Where a teacher reported completing a DIKSHA course without recalling its name, this is treated as confirming one of the two required courses, not both. Confirming both requires evidence of two distinct DIKSHA courses, named or described in the plural. Courses attributed explicitly to iGOT Karmayogi are not counted, since the criteria is DIKSHA-specific.

4 · Findings
This section presents the study's findings intervention wise. For In-Person Training, Digital Courses, and Shaikshik Samvaad, each section is organised into three parts: what is working, what is working with moderate success, and what is not working well. Classroom Observation and Mentoring has not yet been rolled out, so its section is organised slightly differently around what the current baseline shows, what teachers have told us they want, and what needs to be resolved before the intervention launches. The chapter closes with findings that cut across more than one intervention.
Each finding is stated together with the evidence behind it a teacher count, a quoted excerpt, or a comparison against an secondary data source i.e., IPT Subject traininng feedback . Where an independent classroom observation exercise or a platform assessment provides a genuine point of comparison against what teachers reported in interviews, this is stated explicitly and labelled as a triangulated finding. Figures from that observation exercise or from platform data are not presented on their own as findings about teachers; they are used only where they can be compared directly against something teachers said.
4.1 · In-Person Training (IPT)
In-Person Training (IPT) is a subject-specific training delivered to Grade 6–8 teachers in July–August 2025-26, covering Science, Social Science, and Mathematics. Session content centred on orientation to the new NCF-aligned textbooks, subject pedagogy as recommended by the National Curriculum Framework, and lesson planning, with classroom engagement strategies carried over only as brief revision from earlier training cycles. To sustain engagement with this content after the session ended, the programme introduced two follow-up channels which are monthly YouTube Live sessions (YTL) and Sheekh ki Yatra (SKY), a set of online quizzes.
Against this design, EDS examined IPT along six lines of enquiry: how beneficial teachers find the training and what would improve it; what teachers can recall of session content after the fact; what, by their own account, has reached the classroom; what specifically obstructs that transfer; how teachers experience follow-up activity, particularly its online components; and, as a check on the sample itself, how reliably teachers recall their own participation. The findings below are organised around these six lines rather than a single undifferentiated question of whether IPT "worked."
Though as per sample criteria and backend attendnace record all 60 teachers interviews must have attended IPT Subject training. However, 56 teachers reported or confirmed attending subject-specific IPT in 2025–26. One plausible reason could be recall failure due to almost a year time gap.
4.1.1 What is working
Positive perception/Acceptance: Combining data from the EDS exercise and IPT Subject training feedback responses- in general teachers’ perception toward IPT training is positive. It presents as highly acceptable capacity building format and session’s quality have also been rated highly. 52 of 56 reported participating teachers described IPT as useful or helpful. Further, to get more detailed understanding on teachers experience on training quality, teachers feedback data on IPT subject training was evaluated. 70% of 50,899 teachers have rated overall training as highly satisfactory.
However, participation and perception toward extended follow-up activites such as Youtube live, Sheekh ki Yatra have not been equally positive and present a scope for more detailed discussion in below section.
Training learning transfer or implementation in classroom with training material support- Another positive anecdote to explain the training quality and teachers’ enthusiasm, 7 teachers reported applying learnings from their training despite never receiving the Margdarshika or the training slides, and each case is independently confirmed by the note-taker. T21 in Chhindwara is a clear example: he received no material, but during the training session itself he worked through a lesson plan for one topic, and used that same plan in his own classroom afterward. This does not mean materials are unimportant- the wider pattern in this study shows materials are valued where teachers have them. Reading against Sims et al.'s (2025) account of what makes teacher professional development effective, that it must build insight, motivation, technique, and embedded practice, and that insight without technique tends to produce a "knowing-doing gap" : T21's case looks like technique-building happening inside the session itself: working through a lesson plan live, with support, is closer to rehearsal than to content delivery, which would explain why take-home material mattered less to him than to teachers whose only exposure to the technique was watching it explained.
The four most complete transfer cases under IPT identified in this study each combine several techniques rather than one. T18 applied four techniques together- group work, discussion-based teaching, effective homework, and review activities with a stated improvement in participation and learning, independently confirmed by the note-taker. T50 in Ashoknagar combined three techniques with confirmed active use of the Margdarshika. T26 combined four strategies within Science teaching, the most complete case among female teachers in the sample. T38 in Dewas taught conductors and insulators using self-made teaching aids drawn directly from the training. These are the handful cases emerged from this sample.  .
4.1.2 What is working with moderate success
IPT learning transfer: Just over four in ten IPT attendees show a genuinely confirmed transfer case. Applying the same tiered evidence standard as mentioned in section 2.5, 24 of 56 attendees (43%) show a named technique traceable specifically to IPT content: 13 with strong, independently corroborated evidence, 7 with a named technique and application context but less certainty behind it, and 4 with a technique named without enough detail to confirm how or whether it was actually used.
Comparatively moderate success in follow-up activities participation and percception: Teachers participation and engagement with the Follow-up activities like Youtube live (YTL), webex webinar and Sheekh ki Yatra (SKY) have been found moderate and superficial. Participating teacher further don’t report it’s usefulness as highly as for IPT. 21 of 60 teachers attended a YTL follow-up session; of those 21, 16 rated it useful, and 14 specifically described it as helpful for revision. Where teachers elaborate on why, the value is consistently framed as learning reinforcement , which aligned with follow-up’s core objective i.e., as the reiforcer of the IPT learnings. On question of challenge in participation- two teachers, independently of each other, proposed the scheduling fix- suggested moving YTL out of school hours to Saturday afternoon, during the existing baal sabha slot; T37 gave the additional detail of a 2:30–4:00pm window and recommended capping session length at 30–60 minutes.
Lack of content recall and learning transfer: More than half of the attending teachers (30 of 56) were not able to recall anything from the training or follow-up activities.
Need for better training quality: As per IPT subject training, Only 43% rated the training content itself as most relevant and practical for their classroom. 34% rated training infrastructure- sitting arrangements, lunch, and similar logistics below the most-satisfactory level, and 35% rated their own confidence or preparedness to build a lesson plan from the training below the most-satisfactory level.  This sits alongside this study's own finding that IPT is too theory-heavy for a meaningful share of teachers, this large-scale data point toward the same conclusion from a different, much bigger sample: satisfaction with IPT overall is high, but is not the same as confidence that the training translates into classroom-ready practice.
A third of teachers never receive training materials at all. 19 of 60 did not receive the Margdarshika or the training PPT. As per teachers’ perceived value for margdarshika, it will be an important logistic gap to fix in upcoming training cycle. To understand it’s use in training transfer, further CRO data can provide useful indicator.
Training cascade performance, by indicator reported by ~50,899 teachers at the block level cascade IPT training.
4.1.3 What is not working well
Theory-heavy trainings, need for practice time. Eight teachers directly criticise IPT for spending too much time on theory relative to classroom application. The feedback-form data shows this clearly, of the roughly 50,899 teachers who filled out the post-training form, 65% feel highly confident building a lesson plan for students at different levels but only 30% feel that same confidence once the plan has to include real student-engagement strategies. .
"The Content should be focused more on the application of the topics from the subject and less theories, there should be more time allotted for the training."
Two further items on the same feedback form point at the same gap from a different angle. Asked what makes it hardest to teach from a lesson plan they made themselves, 56% of teachers name modifying activities for students at different levels-  more than four times the next most-cited difficulty, completing the plan on time (11%)  and a further 28% name ensuring all students participate. Asked separately what is hardest to retain after any workshop, 36% point to adapting the content to their own classroom, the largest single difficulty on that question (13% cite forgetting the specific technique itself).
Need for contextual and easy to implement contnent: when asked directly what makes classroom implementation difficult- differentiated or mixed-level classrooms are named by 9, time pressure from competing administrative load by 13, and unsatisfactory student attendance or engagement  by 6. A smaller group describes a direct gap between what is demonstrated in training and what a real classroom allows  and further 3 teachers describe a similar difficulty adapting a trained technique to their own classroom. Some of teachers verbatims are as follow-
"classroom mei activity karna hmesha asaan nhi rehta… aap log jo yeh saari gatividhi batate ho khud kaksha mei kar ke dekho pehle…kitni pareshaniyan hai"
"Doing activities in the classroom isn't always easy… you all who teach us these activities should first go do them in the classroom yourselves and see how many difficulties there are."
Another factor that can compromise contextual relevance is when teachers are required to attend training that is not aligned with the subject they teach. T41 reported receiving Science-labelled training content despite teaching Hindi and English, and T7, a Mathematics teacher, said he receives mostly Science-related information because that is the subject WhatsApp group he has been added to. These cases are also the indicative of the gap in training administrative participation or coverage criteria.
Uneven classroom implementation: The classroom observation exercise adds a fourth, independent data point. Of 411 classrooms observed, only 5 % had a written lesson plan on the day of observation, lesson plan development was major content agenda covered in IPT training. This was a single-day snapshot, not a record of habitual practice, and carries the same reliability caveat already stated for this source in Section 2.5. But set against the 65% of teachers who rate themselves highly confident building a lesson plan, it is a wide gap between reported skill and what is observed in the classroom.
Read against Sims et al. (2025), this looks like a technique and embedded-practice gap rather than an insight gap: teachers are not describing confusion about what was taught, but difficulty building it into a plan a real classroom can sustain and that difficulty shows up in the feedback form, in this study's own interviews, and in classroom observation alike. This calls for a stronger need-assessment process to keep training content relevant, and more built-in practice time within the session to build confidence and skill in applying it which is also the direct case for follow-up support developed further in Section 5.1.
Follow-up activity is passive for most of the teachers it reaches. 22 of 60 attended some follow-up activity but recalled no content when probed, a larger group than the 16 who found YTL specifically useful. Sheekh ki Yatra (SKY) reaches far fewer teachers than YTL (9 of 60 vs. 21 of 60) and shows even weaker engagement within that smaller group- most of the 9 only name it without describing any real interaction.
The limitations observed in YTL can be attributed to three distinct reasons-
Scheduling conflicts are the largest cause. T4, T19, and T27 each describe sessions clashing with class time. T27's case is the most complete: blocked from participating both during school hours and after them, leaving no viable window at all.
Absence of monitoring is the second. T21 states plainly that no mechanism tracks YTL attendance or implementation, and that the lack of consequences is why teachers do not take it seriously. T53 makes the same point from the participant's side calling YTL "useful in principle" but deprioritised because it is not checked by officials  and, notably, frames this as "our side problem" rather than assigning blame to the system, an inward-attribution pattern that appears only this once in the dataset.
Access barriers are the third, and are not all the same barrier. T44 never opened YTL links despite seeing them, citing both low digital comfort and a fear that unfamiliar links could be a scam. T55 asks for a low-bandwidth fallback  specifically a printed or PDF version of session content for schools without reliable network. T24 names server-side failures, distinct from local network issues, as a separate barrier to joining.
Dismally low participation in Sheekh ki Yatra: Only 9 out 56 reported to have participated in Sheekh ki Yatra (SKY) quiz series. The thin derived reasons for this can be attributed to the awareness gap and overload or inefficient communication: Two teachers say the information about SKY never reliably reached them, T59 was "not aware of them" at all, and T13 describes the link getting lost among a large volume of other WhatsApp messages. Another teacher mentions explicitly that non-mandatory status of these activities makes them deprioritize YTL or SKY against other competing priorities.
Summary
4.1.4 Implications & Recommendations
Immediate: training design and delivery
The immediate next step is to make sure the training module and its delivery reflect actual classroom contexts. Before the next IPT or follow-up activites cycle is finalised, specific cases, themes, and concerns that teachers are facing in their classrooms should be gathered and built into what the training covers
On delivery, the priority is to build in maximum practice time within the session itself, so teachers leave the training confident about what they're going to do differently and how they're going to do it.
Structural: State–district–block coordination on training infrastructure
This needs a separate, detailed discussion with officials at the state, district, and block level, focused specifically on how the training infrastructure itself can be made better. That conversation should cover:
Clear instructions for each level, so there's no ambiguity about who is responsible for what
A decided indicator for each stakeholder to be held to, so progress can actually be tracked
Adequate budget allocation, confirmed ahead of the cycle rather than resolved mid-way
How all of this is going to be monitored, and at what frequency
Capapcity building support to district, block and cluster level organizer
Follow-up: SKG, YTL, and Webex
The effectiveness of follow-up activity SKG, YTL, and Webex  has not come through as promising in this study, and the design of these needs to be re-evaluated. The core question is how to design follow-up so that teachers keep getting continued support beyond the IPT session itself, rather than a one-off event that fades.
One suggested solution is an integrated IPT–Digital course–CLSS–YTL model, working together as one comprehensive programme design rather than four separate, disconnected activities — this builds on the reinforcement cycle already proposed in Section 6.1, with YTL positioned as the final doubt-clearing layer rather than a standalone follow-up mechanism.
Second, YTL timing needs another look. Teachers currently have to choose between their classroom period and the YTL session, and that's a straightforward scheduling conflict working against attendance — it should be moved to a time that doesn't force that choice, while keeping in mind the equity finding in 4.5.3 that female teachers face specific after-hours constraints, so the new slot needs to work for them too, not just avoid clashing with class time.
Data: quality and implementation indicators
Quality data indicators and classroom implementation indicators need to be fixed across all interventions, so the programme team is actually informed on how each intervention is performing and what teachers are experiencing through the year,not just whether they attended or completed something.
This starts with re-evaluating the existing data capture methods: how reliable they are, how feasible they are to run at scale, and whether they're actually getting us quality data or just a paper trail.
The proposed fix is to initiate FGDs or qualitative interviews with stakeholders in IPT, run alongside the existing online form rather than replacing it, so the detail behind teachers' experiences gets captured, covering online self- reporting forms limitation.
4.2 · Digital Courses (DIKSHA / iGOT Karmayogi)
Digital courses on Diksha are aimed to be training reinforcement in capsule or byte sized modules. In academic year 2025-26, TPD launched roughly four to five DIKSHA courses. Major effort has also been put in course dissemination- strategies to have maximum teacher enrol and complete the course. With this pretext, the EDS set out to understand: general attitude toward their usefulness for professional development; the engagement pattern-  when teachers do courses, for how long, and whether the motivation is self-driven or externally imposed; how much course content teachers actually retain, as a proxy for depth of engagement; feedback on the platform and content, essentially a user survey; feedback on the communication channel through which teachers learn a course exists; and general suggestions for improvement.
Three of these were ECE (early childhood education) courses not designed for this study's target teacher group (middle-grade). The PLC course (Sheekh, Sahyog aur Samvaad) was a pure orientation course introducing the Shaikshik Samvaad platform, not classroom-content instruction. The remaining two courses covered targeted instruction and had significantly low enrolment. As per this context,, DIKSHA could be under-utilised as a channel for teaching-related content specifically for this teacher group over the last academic year  which means a classroom-implementation question does not sit well against most of this course set. The content-recall question remains a fair test, since it asks only whether teachers retain what the course covered, not whether they applied it in the classroom.
Digital courses reach a majority of teachers, but engagement is uneven in ways a completion count alone does not show. Teachers hold a broadly positive view of the format, citing self-paced flexibility and interactive checkpoints as its main strengths, but this attitude does not reliably predict how much of a course's content a teacher retains, a meaningful share complete a course while recalling little or nothing of what it covered. Engagement itself is concentrated outside school hours within a narrow weekly time budget, and for a real minority of teachers is driven by compliance rather than interest. Awareness of any given course travels almost entirely through administrative WhatsApp channels rather than peer networks such as CLSS, despite CLSS being this study's strongest evidence of peer-driven learning elsewhere. Teachers' own feedback on the platform converges on a small number of specific, addressable requests- shorter modules, local examples, and genuine two-way interaction. Below sections segregates findings further with evidences.
4.2.1 What is working
Diksha courses reach: 40 out of 60 (67%) teachers have done some courses on Diksha and 50 (83%) on any platform ( predominately Diksha or iGot). Further enquire on their source of awareness about the particular course launch shows instructions and letters on Jan Shiksha (JSK) Whatsapp group are their major source of information (32.5%) followed by posters on whatsapp groups (JSK or School level).

The single most-liked feature of the platform is its interactivity. 20 teachers specifically praise the mid-course questions, and 12 of them can explain why: a test that will not let you continue until you answer correctly forces you to go back and re-read, and that repetition is what produces recall. Animated videos and pre & post assessments are also well appreciated features in the platform.
What teachers find engaging about DIKSHA courses
Self-paced learning is the  most common reason teachers give for finding digital courses effective. 10 of the 53 teachers who gave a reason for their view on effectiveness pointed to being able to learn at their own pace, in their own time, with content they could revisit the dominant theme in open-ended responses on this question. A further 6 teachers separately credited video and audio formats specifically with making the material easier to learn from.
Teachers’ limited time for digital learning is an important contextual constraint, but existing course duration appears broadly aligned with this constraint. Most teachers (65%) report being able to allocate two hours or less per week to digital learning, including 7 teachers who report no time available, 14 who report one hour, and 18 who report two hours. Since existing digital courses are already designed to be completed within an hour, course length alone does not appear to be a major structural barrier to completion.
Two teachers, independently of each other and of this study's own analysis, proposed solutions this report also recommends. T30 suggested a group-based model for digital courses specifically so that teachers could discuss the content together arrived at on her own, and closely matching the peer-anchored model this report recommends elsewhere. T41 suggested regular monitoring of course progress to encourage genuine completion, directly anticipating the accountability mechanism this report also recommends. Recommendations that a teacher proposes unprompted, in their own words, carry more weight than the same idea proposed by the study team alone.
4.2.2 What is working with moderate success
iGOT Karmayogi has smaller but still meaningful reach: 30 of 60 teachers, against DIKSHA's 40 of 60, and 24 teachers use both platforms. Overall, 50 of 60 completed some digital course in the current cycle once two teachers whose only course history dates to the COVID period are set aside as a historical case rather than current engagement.

DIKSHA and iGOT: Similar Patterns, Different Contexts: DIKSHA and iGOT Karmayogi show a similar pattern of use, but are not treated as equivalent in this report. Both platforms show comparable non-recall issues course completion recorded without content the teacher can describe and neither shows a meaningfully stronger recall rate than the other in this sample. iGOT Karmayogi, however, is not itself a CM RISE TPD intervention; it is a separate national platform that a subset of teachers also happen to use. Its main value to this report is diagnostic rather than programmatic: where teachers describe their iGOT experience, it corroborates or sharpens what is already known about DIKSHA and about digital learning generally for example, the self-paced-format preference and the two-way-interaction complaint both appear on both platforms. This report accordingly does not make iGOT-specific design recommendations; recommendations are directed at DIKSHA and at CM RISE TPD digital delivery, with iGOT evidence used as supporting context only.
Positive perceptions of digital learning not translating into engagement:. Most teachers view digital courses as an effective way to learn, but this positive perception does not consistently translate into meaningful engagement. Of the 60 teachers interviewed, 38 considered digital courses effective, while 8 did not and 6 were unsure. This provides a genuinely positive baseline. However, it sits alongside the finding that a meaningful share of teachers complete courses without being able to describe the content they encountered. This suggests that, for some teachers, “effective” may reflect a general perception of digital learning rather than their experience of engaging with a specific course.
Feedback and suggestions to improve course quality: Where teachers give specific feedback on how to improve course content, four themes recur. Shorter modules are the most common request (4 teachers name a specific length, from 10–45 minutes). A closely related request is for local and subject-specific examples in place of generic content (5 teachers). Offline or low-bandwidth access is named separately from the general connectivity barrier already reported (2 teachers ask specifically for downloadable or PDF content). Two teachers independently describe platform defects- a progress-reset bug and the course-forwarding completion loophole as their top content-quality concern, which suggests these defects register to teachers as a content problem. A substantial share, 9 of the 31 teachers who answered this question, had no specific suggestion to offer.

4.2.3 What is not working well
A third of teachers (20 of 60) reported not doing any courses on DIKSHA at all: upon further enquiry below are the quoted reasons for non-participation: lack of awareness (12), lack of time (10), technical difficulty (8), competing engagement (5), and a view that the content is not useful (1).
Need for strengthening Diksha course communication dissemination: Lack of awareness as the prominent reason for not doing Diksha course raises concern on communication cascade success. Currently, the program uses cascade channel to reach teachers with important information with no mapped tracking where communication fails.
Limited time and competing priorities constrain DIKSHA engagement, despite short course durations. Teachers report having limited time for digital learning most allocate two hours or less per week while also managing other professional learning, including parallel courses on iGOT. Alongside this, lack of time and competing engagements are frequently cited as reasons for not completing DIKSHA courses. This suggests that the challenge is less about course length, as existing courses are already under an hour, and more about teachers having to prioritise which learning activities to engage with within a limited time budget.
Only 12 of 40 participating teachers are able to recall a DIKSHA course topic, name, or specific learning: Even within these 12, the strength of recall varies, as reviewed and confirmed below:
Completion does not reliably mean engagement. 7 teachers reported to have completed a digital course but could recall no content when probed afterward- T10, T13, T16, T40, T44, T53, and T58. A larger and more direct signal sits alongside this: 16 teachers say plainly that they would only take part in a non-mandatory activity if it were made compulsory, and a further 15 show strong self-reported enthusiasm for digital learning paired with a complete inability to describe what they actually learned. T40 in Ratlam put it most directly: “In online courses teachers don't listen carefully, they just complete it for the sake of it.” Set against the platform's own modest pre- to post-test gain (76.74 to 81.63 percent, a delta of approximately 4.90 percentage points) rather than the post-test score alone, this points to a specific and fixable gap: the content works, but a meaningful share of teachers are completing it without engaging, and no current mechanism distinguishes the two.
Teachers' central complaint about the format is the absence of any two-way exchange. Digital learning is repeatedly described as effective channel for receiving information but weak for practice, because there is no space to ask a follow-up question or discuss the content with a peer. This observation has independently been repeated by 6 further teachers in the interviews.

4.2.4  Implications & Recommendations
Awareness and onboarding
Awareness is the single biggest reason teachers haven't done DIKSHA courses- 12 of the 20 teachers with no DIKSHA activity cite lack of awareness, ahead of time constraints (10) and technical difficulty (8) (4.2). This needs a definite, evolving awareness campaign, not a one-time push, since new courses get added through the year and awareness has to keep pace with that. (utilizing whatsapp group)
The channels to run this through are the ones teachers already trust and show up to. CLSS is currently a minor source of DIKSHA information  only 5 of 60 teachers learned about a course through CLSS, against 32 through JSK WhatsApp and 18 through WhatsApp posters (4.2) y. IPT is the other natural fit, since nearly the whole sample passes through it. Both should be used as onboarding points: a short DIKSHA orientation folded into IPT, and course awareness built into the CLSS calendar itself, rather than left to WhatsApp alone.
Course calendar and reinforcement
Establish an annual, byte-sized digital learning calendar with a clear monthly completion target, prioritising a limited number of courses rather than expecting teachers to navigate multiple competing learning demands. Anchor each monthly course to the corresponding CLSS peer discussion through the Blended Samvaad model outlined in Section 6.1. This would give teachers greater clarity on what to prioritise within their limited digital-learning time, while CLSS provides an opportunity to reinforce course content, ask follow-up questions and discuss application with peers. This approach uses existing structures without requiring additional platform functionality.
Coordinated communication campaign
Run a dedicated, coordinated communication campaign around the digital learning calendar, clearly communicating the monthly course priority, expected completion timeline and relevance to teachers’ practice. Consistent messaging across existing communication channels can directly address the reported lack of awareness about DIKSHA courses, while reinforcing which learning activity teachers should prioritise amid competing professional development demands. The campaign should complement not add to the existing volume of teacher communication by providing a single, consistent message around the priority course each month.
Accountability, incentive, and the gendered time gap
An accountability and incentive mechanism for course completion is needed, and needs to be designed together with the gendered time constraint identified in this study, not as a separate fix. 60 percent of teachers report a weekly ceiling of two hours or less for digital learning, and female teachers describe domestic responsibility eating into whatever time is left after school hours, while a comparable male teacher describes no such constraint (4.2, 4.5.3). An accountability mechanism that assumes teachers can simply put in more time outside school hours will land unevenly. Building any incentive structure around a fixed, in-school-hours window  or at minimum an early slot  avoids reproducing that gap.
Platform experience and national coordination
Commission a DIKSHA/iGOT user-experience study covering platform defects, offline access, and connectivity-appropriate formats, going beyond the specific issues already surfaced in this study (like the video-restart defect). This needs to be paired with a standing coordination mandate between the state PMU and the national DIKSHA/iGOT product teams.
Content-specific fixes
Several design fixes fall directly out of what teachers said about the content itself, separate from the platform or the calendar around it.
Font size and mobile-first design. Most teachers doing DIKSHA courses are middle-aged and predominantly using a mobile phone — a smaller screen — to go through the content. Content designed and tested primarily for a laptop or tablet view doesn't carry over well to this reality, and font size is one of the more direct fixes available here.
Audio-visual over text. Teachers consistently prefer short video content over text-heavy modules — 16 teachers express a strong preference for this specifically, consistent with the wider finding that animated videos are the single most-named engaging feature in the dataset, ahead of every other format (4.2). Content built primarily as long-form text works against how teachers are actually engaging with the platform.
More classroom-relevant examples. Teachers want content that solves real classroom challenges, not just conceptual explanation. Practical demonstration videos produce better recall than conceptual lectures- this lines up with the study's own finding that 12 teachers specifically name real classroom examples as an engaging feature, and that self-paced, example-driven content is the leading reason teachers find digital courses effective at all (4.2).
Byte-sized modules. 12 teachers directly request shorter, five-to-ten-minute micro-learning modules, on the grounds that shorter content is easier to sit through and easier to retain. This is a lower cognitive load design, and it's consistent with the course-length complaint already on record — one teacher describes a course as boring specifically because of its length (4.2).
Subject-specific content. Subject-specific digital content has higher perceived utility than generic pedagogy content. Multiple teachers raise this unprompted, asking directly for more subject-specific courses on DIKSHA (4.2) — the same demand these teachers separately raise for CLSS sessions, which suggests it's a preference about the kind of content, not the platform it's delivered on.
Printed reinforcement through CLSS. To reinforce what's covered in the digital module, a one-page printed summary of that month's course can be shared at the same month's CLSS session — an integrated learning model that uses the one channel teachers already trust materials from (4.3) to carry the digital course's content into a format that doesn't depend on a screen at all.
4.3 · Shaikshik Samvaad (CLSS)
CLSS has run in its current cascaded, peer-facilitated format for three years, replacing an earlier model built around one-way YouTube Live sessions. This section examines whether that shift has delivered what it was designed to: teachers rate the format positively and consistently name peer exchange as its core strength, and this perception is backed by the strongest classroom transfer evidence of any intervention in this study — 34 of 53 attendees (64%) show confirmed application of a technique in their own classroom, most often traced to a colleague at the session rather than the facilitator. But three things complicate that picture. First, attendance itself is uneven beneath a reasonably strong headline number: 53 of 60 teachers attend at all, yet fewer than half attend the full annual cycle, and the specific reasons behind the minority who don't attend split cleanly between genuine structural barriers and more addressable ones. Second, the cascade this model depends on to reach teachers who cannot attend a given month, since not every teacher in a school can join monthly, by design  is largely informal and, where it exists at all, difficult to verify independently; a formal, institutional channel is close to absent. Third, teachers who do attend often cannot describe CLSS as a designed, sequenced programme even while valuing individual sessions, suggesting the format's structure is not visible to the people it's built around.
A note on the attendance figure used throughout this section: 53 of 60 teachers attended CLSS, at either the middle or primary level. One of these 53, T60, is a promoted middle-school teacher currently deployed at the primary level; his CLSS attendance was at the primary level only. He is retained in this analysis, including the transfer case drawn from his interview, because the format and facilitation process of CLSS are the same at both levels — but this is flagged here for transparency each time the figure is used.
What is working
Two in three attendees show confirmed classroom transfer. 34 of 53 attendees (64 %) describe a specific technique now used in their classroom, supported by a stated or observed outcome. Within that figure, the evidence is genuinely stronger for some teachers than others: 10 of 53 (19 percent) are independently confirmed by the note-taker or a measurable outcome, a further 17 (combined, 49 percent) are self-reported with credible specific detail, and the remainder name a technique without further detail.

Techniques transferred from CLSS, by frequency
The strongest learnings come from other teachers (Peer learning). At least 4 of the 10 most strongly confirmed CLSS transfer cases traces directly to a peer at the session rather than the facilitator. A teacher interview in Sidhi offers the clearest example: after a peer at a session suggested that all teachers speak to parents together about student attendance, his own classroom attendance rose to roughly 90 percent. This is broadly consistent with research on teacher training elsewhere, which has found that peer support and coaching contribute substantially to whether a new technique is actually used in the classroom, beyond what training content alone achieves.
How CLSS learning currently reaches colleagues who were not there
Teachers are clear and consistent about what makes CLSS work. Peer exchange is the most frequently named strength in the entire dataset (38 teachers), followed by the structure and relevance of session content (19), a trust-based and non-judgemental atmosphere (15 with a further 5 specifically describing reduced inhibition from being treated as a learner rather than an inspected teacher), the generation of new classroom ideas through cross-learning (8), and facilitator quality specifically (4). Put together, these findings describe a consistent pattern in what this group of teachers value about CLSS: peer exchange, psychological safety, and predictable structure, roughly in that order of frequency.
This internal pattern is also consistent with the wider research on what distinguishes an effective professional learning community from one that is simply collegial. Timperley's 2007 Best Evidence Synthesis found that professional learning communities appeared in nearly every successful teacher development intervention reviewed, but participation alone was not sufficient- the communities that changed practice were the ones organised around evidence of what teaching was actually doing to student learning, not simply teachers meeting and talking. Chris Brown's research on professional learning networks makes the same distinction directly: what separates a network that changes classroom practice from one that is only peer collegiality is structure, a shared inquiry focus, and disciplined attention to evidence of impact, rather than trust and camaraderie on their own.
Where teachers have seen the session materials, they value them. 11 of 53 speak positively of the facilitation guide or session content specifically. A related point worth stating plainly: the facilitation guide is, by design, a facilitator-only instrument, not something intended for general teacher circulation — so this figure should be read as a sign that materials are valued where teachers encounter them, not as evidence of a distribution gap.
4.3.2 What is working with moderate success
A third of attendees engage with the full annual cycle. 20 of 53 attended all five sessions in the year. a meaningful core group, though the majority attend some but not all sessions. The distribution below this headline figure is informative on its own: 23 of 53 attendees (43%) attended three sessions or fewer across the year (1 session: 4 teachers; 2: 6; 3: 13), against 20 of 53 who attended all five. Participation is not simply full-cycle or absent — a substantial middle group attends roughly half the year's sessions or less, which is a meaningfully weaker dose coupled with less formalized learning cascade system than the headline attendance figure alone suggests.
Weaker recall on the topic or themes of the attended CLSS: A separate question on topic recall provides a more direct measure of what teachers retained from CLSS. Of the 53 teachers reported attending CLSS, 18 could not recall any specific CLSS topic. Among those who could recall a topic, classroom management was the most frequently retained theme, followed by effective homework and prior-knowledge/group-work techniques.
Well working cascaded communication across district- cluster and school level for CLSS- Most non-attendance is not, in itself, an awareness problem. Among the small number of teachers who did not attend CLSS, most already know it exists and understand its basic format. Quoted challenges on participation are mostly around structural such as lack of transportation, health issue, single teacher at school etc.
Teachers generally recall the RSK MP platform as the attendance channel, but the underlying measurement is limited. 45 of 53 asked teachers say they can recall RSK MP as the platform used for CLSS attendance. This figure fsirly establishes that the awareness issue is not the reason for low attendance from CLSS. Shared reasons for this are shared in the below section.
What is not working well
Most teachers cannot reliably describe CLSS as a designed, structured programme. Reflecting on their own batch, 11 of 20 interviewers report that most of their respondents could recall CLSS's structure and format — its fixed Saturday scheduling, the agenda-based session design, and the design principles behind it. 7 of 20 report that most respondents could not. The remaining 2 did not ask about or observe this directly, and are recorded as such rather than folded into either side.
This sits alongside a separate, more clear-cut finding from the same debrief: unaided recognition of CLSS itself is near-universal — all 20 of 20 interviewers report their respondents recognised the programme by name and could confirm attendance without prompting. Read together, this describes a programme that is very well recognised by name and attendance, but whose underlying design is understood by a clear majority of respondents in barely more than half of interview batches (11 of 20), and not at all in over a third (7 of 20).
Formal cascade,  a named, institutional channel for spreading learning is almost entirely absent. Only two teachers, T5 and T28, show a school staff meeting genuinely used as a cascade channel in practice, with T28's case independently confirmed. A further eight teachers or observers describe staff meetings as a channel that could be used but currently is not — a real signal of unmet demand for a formal mechanism, not evidence that one already exists.
Where independent observation exists, it more often questions what teachers report about cascade than confirms it. Only 21 of 60 teachers have a note-taker observation directly relevant to cascade, and of those 21,  two in three use language that hedges, doubts, or directly contradicts what the teacher said. One teacher contradicted himself within the same interview, first denying any cascade took place and then describing having shared it. Another claimed both a WhatsApp group and verbal cascade were in use, while the note-taker recorded plainly that no structured process was observed. The pattern is consistent enough that self-reported cascade activity should be treated as an upper estimate, not a confirmed practice.
The post-session attendance and feedback process creates real friction for a meaningful share of teachers. 12 of 60 cite the rush to leave the venue at the end of a session, 8 cite general awareness issues, 8 describe unwillingness, and 4 say the form is not seen as important. A separate group of 34 teachers selected “Others” on this question, but the underlying data shows this is largely a gap in the survey design rather than 34 additional distinct reasons: 30 of the 34 simply repeated the word “others” with no further detail, and no “please specify” follow-up was ever captured for this option.
A small number of teachers face a genuine structural barrier to attending CLSS at all. Three teachers face a barrier that is structural in the clearest sense: physical disability preventing travel, health leave, and a school-level staffing shortage that leaves no one to cover their class, respectively. No catch-up route currently exists for any of the three. A further four non-attendees face a different kind of barrier — not being instructed to attend, low motivation, or confusion between CLSS and a different training format — and this distinction matters for design, because a catch-up route addresses the first group but would do nothing for the second.
4.3.4 Implications & Recommendations
Making the structure visible:
CLSS is well recognised by name and attendance and perceived value for the intervention is also high. However, a meaningful share of teachers cannot describe it as a designed, structured programme. The interviewer debrief on this point puts the number at 7 of 20 interview batches where most respondents could not recall CLSS's structure or annual cycle, with a further 11 of 20 where most could. This needs more emphasised, targeted communication to participating teachers on what CLSS actually is-  the fixed Saturday scheduling, the agenda behind each session, complaince on sticking to the agenda and how sessions build across the year.
A formal structure and process for the CLSS cascade:
Alongside this, introduce a one-page teacher-facing CLSS session summary, distinct from the facilitation guide. The facilitation guide is a facilitator-only instrument by design and was never meant for general circulation (4.3), which is part of why teachers currently have nothing of their own to anchor the year-long structure to. A short summary what this session covered, how it connects to the last one and the next — gives teachers something concrete to hold onto between session
Fixing cascade channel and verification together
Formal cascade to colleagues who didn't attend is almost entirely absent in practice: only 2 teachers (T5, T28) reported about formal staff-meeting channel used to share CLSS learning with other teachers. This needs to be structured formally and deliberately: a defined path from the attending teacher to their colleagues.  Further asistance can be provided by making it madatory to share one pager summary of the session to all teachers school level whatsapp group.
Continuity between sessions Open each CLSS session with a review of the previous month's implementation, and use this moment to surface peer examples deliberately rather than leaving peer exchange to happen informally. This addresses the continuity gap directly: teachers currently experience each session in isolation, without a sense of how it fits into a year-long design, and a teacher who doesn't know sessions are building toward something is less likely to prepare for the next one or connect it to the last. It also reinforces the study's own strongest finding — that peer-sourced technique is the mechanism behind CLSS's best transfer outcomes (4.3) — by giving that exchange a fixed, recurring place in the session rather than relying on it to happen on its own.
Catch-up routes for structural exclusion
Physical disability preventing travel, health leave, and a staffing shortage with no cover, respectively (4.3, 5.2). No catch-up route currently exists for any of them. This is a small, well-defined group, and a catch-up mechanism should be built specifically for this barrier type distinct from the other non-attendance reasons below, which call for a different kind of fix.
The attendance/feedback form
setting aside the last 15 minutes of every session specifically for the form, and tying it to attendance itself so it isn't treated as optional- a teacher's attendance only counts as complete once the form is submitted. For teachers who can't get the government portal to work on the spot, there needs to be a fallback: an additional Google Form, or a hardcopy version they can fill on paper and hand in, rather than being left out simply because the portal failed them that day. On the facilitator side, their own digital form should carry a field to flag any technical issue a teacher ran into while filling theirs, so a portal problem gets recorded as a portal problem, not folded into "unwillingness."
None of this holds without follow-through at the cluster level. A stronger accountability and monitoring system is needed to track each cluster's performance on attendance and feedback-form completion, with clusters held to and incentivised against this protocol, rather than leaving it to individual teacher compliance. And this shouldn't only track where it's failing: good cases, where feedback actually fed back into a better CLSS experience, should be surfaced and highlighted, so the form is visibly connected to something changing, not just a box to tick.

4.4  Classroom Observation and Mentoring (CRO)
Because CRO is pre-rollout, this section is organised differently from the three sections before it. Instead of what is working, moderate, and not working, it covers what the current baseline shows, what teachers have told us they want from the intervention, and what needs to be resolved before it launches.
4.4.1 What the current baseline shows
i. Almost no mentoring-style observation currently reaches middle-grade teachers, and where classroom observation does happen, teachers associate it with inspection rather than support. Most schools report no mentoring visit in the current academic year, and teachers link classroom observation mainly to BAC and CAC compliance visits. 12 of 60 do not see classroom observation as a form of professional development at all.
ii. This baseline carries a specific risk for the rollout. The officials now being trained to conduct CRO visits are, in many cases, the same officials teachers currently associate with inspection- a structural overlap that creates a real risk of role conflation unless training and rollout make the distinction explicit (Section 6, recommendation 16).
Separately, awareness of what a supportive observation practice would even look like remains thin, including among engaged teachers. T35 in Chhatarpur — a 13-year teacher with confirmed CLSS transfer, not someone disengaged from professional development generally — put it plainly: "I don't know about mentoring. There is no set classroom observation practice." This suggests the gap is not only about how CRO will be perceived once it arrives, but whether teachers currently have any clear picture of it at all.
4.4.2 What teachers say they want
Teachers converge on a small number of clear, specific preferences for what a supportive observation should look like — useful because CRO's design is not yet finalised, so this evidence can shape it directly rather than only critique it after the fact.
“aate hain, peeche baithte hain aur sahayta karte hain.”
  They come, sit at the back, and help.
  — T56, Sehore
iii. One dissenting view is worth recording on its own: T59 in Vidisha argued that some degree of accountability pressure is necessary for teachers to perform well — the only teacher in the sample of 60 to take this position. The dominant preference is clearly for dignified, non-punitive feedback, but this single view is a reminder that the preference is not universal.
4.4.3 What needs to be resolved before rollout
The inspection–mentoring distinction needs to be built into official training explicitly, given that the same officials already carry out compliance visits.
No follow-through mechanism currently exists. Nothing in the current landscape keeps a shared record of what was observed, what was agreed, or what will be tried next — this needs to be designed in from the start, not added after the fact.
The co-teaching request from 10 teachers is a genuinely different model from observe-and-feedback, and is worth a deliberate design decision rather than being folded in as a minor variation.
4.5 · Cross-Cutting Findings
The findings in this section are not specific to any one intervention. Several of them explain a pattern that shows up in more than one of the sections above; others compare what teachers reported in interviews against another source of intervention’s data such as the classroom observation exercise done in 2025 or the RSK backend atrendance record to present well rounded information.
4.5.1  The post-delivery gap
The same structural gap recurs across every intervention: The program has the data till delivery complaince attendance, cicular and letters) and to some extent- about the quality of particular event through feedback form (CLSS and IPT trainings). However, these give the limited scope to unpack the detail on implementation and further intervention’s journey to classroom . In this study, we found the gap in recall, delivery happens, but little connects it to what happens afterward. 7 teachers raised this gap without being asked, across 7 districts — not a pattern the study went looking for, but one teachers volunteered on their own. 22 of 60 attended IPT follow-up activity but could not recall its content afterward. The same shape of gap appears in digital courses (7 of 60 complete a course with no recall) and, as established in the previous section, in CLSS as well (6 teachers). One reasonable way to read these four patterns together is as a single structural gap appearing in four different forms, rather than four unrelated problems — though this study's design cannot rule out that each has a partly distinct cause specific to its intervention.
4.5.2  Recorded participation and classroom reality
Across every intervention, what is recorded as participation tends to overstate what is actually happening in the classroom. A CLSS co-facilitator, T46, shows no transfer at all despite full attendance at every session. The gap also appears among teachers with the strongest recorded engagement: 5 teachers in the highest engagement category show no evidence of transfer despite a complete participation record (T4, T51, T27, T32, T25). This matters for how engagement is measured going forward — a high participation count is not, on its own, evidence that learning reached the classroom.
Three independent measurement approaches – IPT & CLSS self reported attendance, Diksha digital course-completion records, and recall testing show the pattern of significant gap in participation translating into classroom transfer.

The gendered time constraint: Female teachers describe domestic responsibility limiting how much time they have for digital learning and follow-up engagement outside school hours, while a comparable male teacher describes no equivalent constraint. This is consistent with the separate, direct finding that 48 of 60 teachers do their digital learning specifically after school hours (4.2), when that time is least evenly available. Three teachers (T44, T47, and T58) face a barrier that is structural in the : physical disability preventing travel, health leave, and a school-level staffing shortage that leaves no one to cover their class, respectively. No catch-up route currently exists for any of the three. A further four non-attendees face a different kind of barrier that is not being instructed to attend, low motivation, or confusion between CLSS and a different training format
4.5.4  Motivation
Teachers with intrinsic motivation are more likely to show genuine transfer. Roughly 48 percent of the sample is intrinsically motivated, and nearly all of the strongest CLSS transfer cases come from this group. T31 is a clean individual example: an independently confirmed technique transfer sits alongside an independently confirmed intrinsic orientation — the note-taker's own observation, made without prompting, was that “new learning is her priority.” The same pattern holds across very different career stages: T24, with 43 years of experience, shows the same kind of self-directed, non-compliance-driven engagement with professional development as T12, a teacher with three years of experience, described elsewhere in this study.
Teachers motivated mainly by compliance are more likely to show the participation-reality gap. 10 percent of the sample is compliance-primary, and all four teachers in this group show a confirmed participation-reality gap. This is an association in a small group of four teachers, not proof that compliance causes the gap, and it should be weighted accordingly. It is, however, consistent with a broader and well-established body of motivation research — self-determination theory, developed by Deci and Ryan and tested extensively since — which has found that autonomous, self-directed engagement tends to produce more durable behaviour change than engagement driven mainly by external requirement. That research was not conducted on this population and cannot substitute for direct evidence from it, but it offers a plausible account for why the pattern in this small group might hold more broadly, and is offered here as context rather than confirmation.
4.5.5  Triangulated insight: self-report compared with independent classroom observation
A second triangulation is possible for IPT specifically, against the separate feedback exercise covering 50,899 teachers introduced in Section 4.1. This study's own interviews found a small number of teachers (8 of 60) describing IPT as too theory-heavy relative to classroom practice — a real but modest signal on its own. The larger exercise, asked of a different and much bigger sample immediately after training rather than months later, shows a consistent pattern at scale: teachers rate the training highly satisfying and the module content highly understandable (70% and 92% respectively at the top rating), but only 30% rate themselves highly confident applying engagement strategies in their own lesson plans, and only 22% highly confident mentoring another teacher on the same content. The two data sources, collected independently and at very different scales, point toward the same underlying gap — between understanding training content and being able to use or transmit it — which is a materially stronger basis for this finding than either source offers alone.
IPT feedback exercise: comprehension versus application (50,899 teachers)
Year 2025-26 classroom observation exercise from 415 classrooms, drawn from a wider sample of master trainers offers a genuine check on what teachers reported about their own technique use in interviews. The comparison below uses this independent data only where it can be set directly against something teachers said; the figures were re-verified against the exercise's raw, classroom-by-classroom data during this analysis.
The direction of the gap is not consistent, which is itself informative. Cold Calling and Group Discussion are reported more often than they are observed; the Entry Hook and Constructive Feedback are observed more often than they are reported. A single explanation teachers simply overstating their own practice does not fit this pattern on its own, since it would predict every technique moving in the same direction. This biirectional pattern is not unusual: a substantial body of education research has found that teacher self-report of specific classroom behaviours cannot be assumed to match what an observer records, in either direction, and the mismatch is well-documented enough that it is treated as a standing methodological caution rather than a surprising result. One plausible explanation for the specific pattern seen here, though this study cannot confirm it directly, is that teachers report accurately on techniques they consciously associate with a named training session, while genuinely good practice they do not attribute to any specific training goes unreported. If this explanation holds, it points to two separate design tasks: closing the gap for named, trained techniques, and helping teachers recognise good practice they already show without realising it counts but this report treats that as a hypothesis worth testing further, not an established finding.
4.5.6  The EDS sampling design
The engagement categories used to design this study's sample match what field interviews found less than half the time. 24 of 60 teachers (40 percent) match their assigned engagement category exactly. The largest source of mismatch is measurement, not a real participation gap: the digital criterion is defined by two named DIKSHA courses (PLC and PSP4), not a general count of digital activity, and course-name recall proved fragile across the sample — only one High-category teacher confirms the two distinct courses their tier requires, even though 40 of 60 teachers show some DIKSHA activity on record. Full methodology and the complete teacher-by-teacher comparison are in Section 3. Separately, six teachers show substantially more engagement — including 3 to 5 CLSS sessions with confirmed transfer — than their backend category would predict. Both findings point toward the same conclusion: future engagement categorisation should incorporate transfer quality and motivation type, and should not rely on course-name recall as the sole test of digital completion, rather than participation counts alone.
4.5.7  System-level barriers named by teachers
Teachers themselves argue that professional development cannot succeed without broader system reform. 11 teachers state directly that PD effectiveness depends on fixes that sit outside any single intervention's design staffing levels, workload, and syllabus simplification among them. This is a structural observation, not a criticism of any one programme, and is worth reading as context for every recommendation in this report.
A related but distinct pattern: some teachers receive programme information but cannot act on it. 6 teachers receive programme information through WhatsApp but cannot act on it because of school staffing constraints- a capacity gap, separate from an awareness gap, and one that closing the awareness gap alone will not fix.
Finally, COVID-era digital engagement, beyond the two teachers already excluded from the current-cycle digital count elsewhere in this report, appears not to have converted into lasting practice more broadly: 10 teachers show a wider pattern of high course completion during the COVID period that did not carry forward into ongoing engagement, a caution against assuming past digital activity is a reliable predictor of current engagement.
5 · Cross-Cutting Analysis
5.1  The Participation-Reality Gap as a system property
The most consequential analytical point in this study is that the participation-reality gap should be read as a property of the system's measurement approach, not as a shortcoming of individual teachers. The same pattern recurs across interventions: recorded participation, whether attendance, completion, or category, consistently overstates classroom reality. Seven teachers completed digital courses with no recall. Five High-category teachers show no evidenced transfer. A CLSS co-facilitator attended all five sessions and showed no transfer at all.
What connects these cases is not dishonesty or disengagement on the part of teachers, but that the instruments currently used to track TPD measure inputs rather than outcomes. Attendance registers record presence. Completion flags record that a course was marked finished. Engagement categories record counts. None of these measures learning, and none measures classroom change. The gap follows predictably from a measurement approach that stops at the point of delivery. This reframing points to a fixable cause: not teacher motivation, which is difficult to influence directly, but the design of what the system chooses to measure, which is not.
The scale of the separate IPT feedback exercise (Section 4.1) makes it possible to describe this gap's extent more precisely for one intervention, and to point toward a plausible cause rather than only naming the pattern. At a system level, teachers report broadly high satisfaction and comprehension: 70% rate overall training highly satisfactory, and 92% rate the module itself understandable. But two more specific questions in the same exercise, asked of the same 50,899 teachers, show a much weaker result. Only 30% place themselves at the top comfort level for building a lesson plan using effective student engagement strategies specifically — as against comprehension of the training content, this is a question about being able to use it. More directly relevant to cascade: only 22% place themselves at the top comfort level for mentoring or training other teachers on the same strategies. This is not a recall question, and it was asked of teachers immediately after training, not months later — so it cannot be explained by the passage of time the way this study's own interview-based recall gap can. Taken together, the two data points suggest a specific and fixable barrier behind the wider participation-reality gap: teachers can find a training satisfying and understandable while still not feeling equipped to apply it independently or explain it to a colleague, and the second of those two barriers speaks directly to why formal cascade to non-attending teachers is so consistently thin across the interventions in this study, not only IPT.
This low-confidence data point is more than a standalone finding — it plausibly explains the non-recall pattern documented throughout this report, rather than sitting alongside it as a separate issue. A teacher who does not feel equipped to apply a strategy independently is less likely to actually try it in the classroom, and a strategy never tried is a strategy with nothing to recall later — which reframes the recall gap as a downstream symptom of an implementation gap, not only a memory or monitoring problem. This builds a direct case for follow-up support: IPT’s current duration and structure does not appear to leave teachers with enough confidence for reliable classroom transfer on its own, which is exactly the gap a well-designed follow-up mechanism is positioned to close. The same logic extends to CLSS cascade: its thinness is not fully explained by the absence of a formal mechanism, since a motivated teacher without a  mechanism has been shown elsewhere in this study to find an informal one. The mentoring-confidence figure suggests willingness is a second, independent constraint — consistent with the recurring pattern across interventions that teachers engage most reliably with what they are directly instructed to do, and disengage where an activity is optional and requires them to take initiative alone.

6 · Implications and Recommendations
The recommendations in this section are organised around a single structural choice: strengthening and connecting the follow-up mechanisms that already reach teachers, namely CLSS, digital courses, structured follow-up activity, and CRO mentoring, rather than treating each as a separate channel requiring its own isolated fix. This follows directly from the finding in Section 5.1 that the participation-reality gap is a property of the system's measurement and reinforcement architecture rather than a shortcoming specific to any one intervention. Section 6.1 sets out an integrated reinforcement model built on this logic. Section 6.2 addresses a more immediate need: since this year's IPT design is already finalised, the priority for the current cycle is to improve what is captured about it rather than to redesign it. Section 6.3 covers platform experience and coordination. Section 6.4 presents the full action list with sequencing.
6.1  In-person Training (IPT)
YTL could potentially move to the existing Saturday baal sabha slot (2:30–4:00pm). YTL already reaches 21 of 60 teachers, and 14 of 60 find it useful specifically for revision, but current follow-up activity is largely passive and easy to skip. Two teachers in this study independently proposed this same scheduling idea. Shifting it to an existing slot could help address the barrier teachers describe  being forced to choose between a classroom period and a YTL session without asking for a new time commitment from anyone.
It may help to supplement the online post-training form with FGDs or in-depth interviews at randomly sampled training batches. This study's own findings suggest some risk in relying on a single self-report instrument, and recall bias appears to become a real factor once teachers are surveyed five to eleven months after the training event in question. Adding qualitative depth at a sample of batches not necessarily every batch could offer a way to check the form's findings against something else.
Training-quality indicators and teacher experience could potentially be captured during the training itself, rather than only afterward. This connects to the same recall-bias consideration behind 9b: post-hoc data collection depends on whatever a teacher can still remember months later, which this study suggests is often not much. Capturing quality data closer to the point of training, while the experience is still fresh, may be a more reliable approach than trying to correct for recall gaps afterward.
Recall-reinforcement elements might be worth building into IPT session design itself. Follow-up activity alone (YTL, Webex, SKY) may not be enough to compensate for weak retention if the original session doesn't build in some repetition or reinforcement. Structured recap points within the training itself, rather than relying entirely on a follow-up call weeks later, could give content a better chance of sticking before a teacher leaves the room.
A 30-day implementation check after IPT could be a useful addition. Seven teachers in this study raised a post-delivery gap unprompted — without being asked directly, they independently described training that was never followed up on. A short check-in around a month after training, timed to when a teacher would realistically have had a chance to apply something, could speak directly to the gap these teachers described in their own words.
Training materials could be distributed at the IPT venue itself, on Day 1. Nineteen of sixty teachers in this study report never receiving training materials at all-  a meaningful minority left without anything to refer back to afterward. Distributing materials at the venue directly, rather than through a separate channel later, may help close the most obvious point where this gap currently occurs.
It could help to systematically gather classroom-context cases ahead of each IPT cycle, and to weight session design toward hands-on practice time. Eight teachers in this study describe IPT as too theory-heavy relative to actual classroom application. Collecting real classroom scenarios before designing the next cycle, and leaning sessions more toward practice, would speak directly to this specific feedback rather than assuming training generally needs more of something.
A structured infrastructure discussion with state, district, and block officials may be worth considering. A separate 50,899-teacher feedback exercise found 34% rating training infrastructure below most-satisfactory — a pattern this study's 60 interviews alone couldn't fully capture but appear to support. Such a discussion could usefully cover indicators, budget allocation, and monitoring responsibility at each level, since infrastructure quality likely depends on coordination that a training-design fix alone may not resolve.
Sheekh ki Yatra (SKY)'s design and its positioning relative to YTL could be worth reviewing before further investment. SKY currently reaches far fewer teachers than YTL (9 of 60 against 21 of 60) and appears to show weaker engagement where it does. Rather than assuming more investment would close this gap on its own, it may be worth first understanding whether the gap comes from SKY's design or its positioning alongside YTL
6.2 Digital Courses
An annual, byte-sized digital course calendar with a completion target, sequenced to IPT priority areas, could be a useful structure to introduce. A post-delivery gap appears across all four interventions in this study, not just digital courses, and performative completion — finishing a course without really engaging with it — seems linked to a compliance-only framing for a minority of teachers. A predictable calendar might give teachers a clearer target than ad hoc course launches, and sequencing it to IPT's priorities could help digital learning reinforce training rather than compete with it for attention.
An accountability and incentive mechanism for digital course completion may be worth designing. Compliance-only motivation appears connected to this study's participation-reality gap, and 14 of 60 teachers complete a course with no recall afterward. Any such mechanism would likely benefit from being designed alongside the gendered time constraint this study also surfaces — an incentive that assumes teachers can simply find more time outside school hours risks landing unevenly across genders.
A DIKSHA and iGOT user-experience study, covering classroom and geographic context and not only known defects, could be worth commissioning. This study independently confirmed a video-restart defect and surfaced onboarding and navigation friction that hasn't been formally diagnosed. A dedicated UX study could go beyond the specific bugs this study happened to surface, looking at the platform experience more broadly across the range of contexts teachers use it in.
A standing coordination arrangement between the state PMU and the national DIKSHA technical and PMU teams may be worth establishing. Platform defects, offline access, and dashboard granularity all appear to need resolution at the national level, beyond what the state programme can address alone. A standing relationship, rather than an ad hoc request each time something breaks, could give the state a more reliable channel for this kind of fix.
6.3 Shaikshik Samwaad (CLSS)
Each CLSS session could potentially open with a short review of the previous month's implementation. No continuity mechanism currently seems to connect one CLSS session to the next, so each month may start fresh rather than building on what teachers tried since the last one. A brief review at the start — what worked, what didn't — could help turn CLSS into more of a connected annual cycle, and give teachers a reason to have tried something in between sessions.
It may help to structure the school-level cascade from the attending teacher to their colleagues. Cascade to non-attending colleagues currently seems to happen mostly on individual initiative, with limited institutional backing — this study found only two confirmed cases of a genuine staff-meeting cascade mechanism. A more structured process, even a simple one, could help make this reach more consistent across a school, rather than depending on which teachers happen to be inclined to share.
Communicating CLSS's structure and annual cycle to participating teachers could be worth prioritising. A notable pattern in this study: about half of interviewed teachers cannot describe CLSS as a designed, sequenced programme, even though they attend and value individual sessions. Teachers seem to know the parts but not always the whole. Explaining the annual structure — what's covered when, and why, that could be a relatively low-cost way to close this specific gap.
A one-page teacher-facing CLSS session summary might be a useful addition. The facilitation guide is a facilitator-only instrument by design, which may leave teachers without much of their own to hold onto after a session. A short summary — what this session covered, how it connects to last month and next month — could give teachers something concrete of their own, addressing a similar gap to recommendation 7 through a different channel.
Catch-up routes for teachers facing a structural barrier could be worth introducing. Three teachers in this study face what looks like a genuinely structural barrier to CLSS attendance — disability, health leave, or a staffing shortage with no one to cover their class — and no alternative pathway currently seems to exist for any of them. It's worth noting a catch-up route would likely help this specific group but may do little for the larger group of non-attendees whose barrier isn't structural, which would probably need a different approach.
6.4 Classroom Observation and Teacher Mentoring
Building the inspection–mentoring distinction into CRO official training could be a useful starting point. Twelve of sixty teachers do not currently see classroom observation as a form of professional development at all, and this may partly be structural — the same officials often carry out both compliance inspections and mentoring visits. Training officials explicitly on this distinction seems like a prerequisite worth considering before any protocol design choice can be expected to help much on its own.
Dignity, solution-focus, and subject relevance could be worth encoding into the CRO protocol directly. Across sixty interviews, teachers converge on much the same three preferences for what a supportive observation might look like: private and respectful feedback, guidance focused on solutions rather than evaluation, and an observer credible in the teacher's own subject. Since CRO's design isn't finalised yet, this could be a good opportunity to let teacher evidence shape the protocol directly, rather than only reviewing it after the fact.

6.5 Cross-cutting
Anchoring each month's digital course theme in that month's CLSS session — a Blended Samvaad model — could be worth exploring. Peer exchange is the reported source for 5 of the 9 strongest CLSS transfer cases, and teachers separately point to the lack of two-way interaction as digital learning's biggest weakness. Routing the month's digital theme through CLSS might give digital content the peer discussion it currently lacks, without requiring anything new to be built on the platform itself.
The reinforcement cycle could potentially be extended to explicitly include YTL, as part of an integrated IPT–Digital Course–CLSS–YTL model. Follow-up support currently seems to operate as separate, disconnected channels rather than one coherent design, a pattern this study documents throughout. Bringing YTL into the same reinforcement cycle already suggested for digital courses and CLSS (recommendation 2) could help close the loop across all three, rather than treating each as its own isolated fix.
7 · Proposal for the Next Phase
This diagnostic documents the TPD ecosystem from the perspective of teachers. The concept note anticipated a fuller diagnosis that would also capture the perspective of academic officials across the system, and several findings in this report make that second phase a clear next step rather than an optional extension.
Why an officials-side study is needed
A number of findings in this study point to a delivery chain that could only be observed from one end. Teachers describe materials that did not arrive, follow-up sessions they were never informed of, CLSS structure that was not cleared to them, and observation visits that read as inspection. Each of these is a symptom whose cause lies somewhere in the chain between programme design and classroom delivery. A study that hears only the teacher's account can describe these symptoms accurately without identifying where in the chain they originate.
Proposed design
Two further extensions are worth considering alongside the officials-side study. The first is a dedicated DIKSHA user experience study, described in the priority action list above, to diagnose platform friction that this phase could identify only as symptoms. The second is limited independent observation of a sub-sample of interviewed teachers, since the current classroom observation exercise covers a different set of teachers and this phase's Tier 2 and Tier 3 transfer findings remain dependent on self-report; a small paired observation exercise would allow future work to raise confidence in those tiers specifically.
